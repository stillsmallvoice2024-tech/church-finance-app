# Module 1: Money in Trust

## The First Principle of Financial Stewardship

Before we talk about spreadsheets, software, or even numbers — we need to talk about what money in an organization actually *is*.

When someone gives to a church, a ministry, or an NGO, they are not making a purchase. They are placing their trust in you. The money they give is not yours — it is held in trust, on behalf of a mission, on behalf of a community, often on behalf of God himself.

This is called **stewardship** — and it changes everything about how money should be handled.

---

## The Stewardship Principle

In accounting, the foundational idea is this:

> **An organization exists separately from the people who run it.**

This is called the **Entity Concept** — one of the most important principles in accounting. The organization's money is not the treasurer's money. It is not the pastor's money. It is not the board's money. It belongs to the entity, held for its stated mission.

This has practical consequences:
- Personal and organizational finances must never be mixed
- Every financial decision must be traceable back to the organization's purpose
- Records must be kept as if anyone could ask to see them — because they can

---

## Why Records Are Not Optional

Many mission-driven organizations start with informal systems: a notebook, a shared spreadsheet, a memory. This works — until it doesn't.

Without proper records:
- You cannot tell donors how their gifts were used
- You cannot spot errors, fraud, or misuse
- You cannot plan a budget against real numbers
- You cannot produce a report for your board
- You cannot survive a financial review or audit

The absence of records is not just an inconvenience — it is a failure of stewardship. It breaks trust.

---

## The Accounting Equation

Every financial system in the world — from the simplest church ledger to the most complex corporate ERP — rests on one equation:

```
Assets = Liabilities + Equity (Net Assets)
```

In plain English:
- **Assets** — everything the organization owns or is owed (cash, bank balances, equipment)
- **Liabilities** — everything the organization owes to others (debts, unpaid bills)
- **Equity / Net Assets** — what's left after obligations are met; for non-profits this is called *net assets* or *fund balance*

This equation must always balance. If it doesn't, something is wrong.

---

## What Good Financial Stewardship Looks Like

An organization with healthy financial stewardship can answer these questions at any time:

1. How much money do we have, and where is it?
2. Where did the money come from?
3. Where did the money go?
4. Are we staying within our budget?
5. Can we account for every designated gift?
6. Who approved each transaction?

If any of these questions takes more than a few minutes to answer, the financial system needs work.

# Module 2: The Language of Money

## Why Accounting Has Its Own Vocabulary

Every profession has a language. Accounting is no different. The good news: once you understand a handful of terms, the rest falls into place naturally.

This module gives you the vocabulary you need to understand your organization's finances — and to follow the rest of this course.

---

## Inflows and Outflows

The most basic distinction in any financial system:

| Term | What it means | Also called |
|---|---|---|
| **Inflow** | Money coming into the organization | Income, Revenue, Receipt |
| **Outflow** | Money leaving the organization | Expense, Payment, Disbursement |

For a church or ministry, inflows typically include:
- Tithes and offerings
- Designated donations (for specific projects or purposes)
- Grants
- Fundraising proceeds

Outflows typically include:
- Staff salaries and wages
- Utilities and building maintenance
- Programme and ministry costs
- Administrative expenses

**Why it matters:** Every transaction is either an inflow or an outflow. Knowing which is which is the starting point of every financial record.

---

## Accounts and Bank Accounts

In accounting, an **account** is a named container that tracks a specific type of financial activity. Think of it like a labelled bucket.

There are five main types of accounts:

| Account Type | Tracks | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Asset** | What you own or are owed | Bank balances, cash, receivables |
| **Liability** | What you owe | Loans, unpaid invoices |
| **Equity / Net Assets** | Remaining value after obligations | Fund balance, retained surplus |
| **Income** | Money received | Tithes, donations, grants |
| **Expense** | Money spent | Salaries, rent, utilities |

A **bank account** is a specific type of asset account — it represents real money held at a financial institution. Most organizations have more than one: a main operating account, a savings account, perhaps a foreign currency account.

---

## Debits and Credits

This is where many people get confused — but it is simpler than it sounds.

**Double-entry bookkeeping** is the system used by every serious financial organization in the world. Its core rule:

> **Every transaction affects at least two accounts. Every debit has a corresponding credit.**

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| **Debit** | An entry on the left side of an account |
| **Credit** | An entry on the right side of an account |

The effect of a debit or credit depends on the account type:

| Account Type | Debit effect | Credit effect |
|---|---|---|
| Asset | Increases (+) | Decreases (−) |
| Liability | Decreases (−) | Increases (+) |
| Equity / Net Assets | Decreases (−) | Increases (+) |
| Income | Decreases (−) | Increases (+) |
| Expense | Increases (+) | Decreases (−) |

**Example:** A donation of ₦50,000 is received into the church's bank account.
- Debit: Bank Account (Asset) +₦50,000
- Credit: Tithe Income (Income) +₦50,000

Both sides balance. The equation holds.

---

## Cash Basis vs. Accrual Basis

There are two ways to decide *when* to record a transaction:

**Cash basis accounting** — record when money actually changes hands.
- Simple, easy to understand
- Common in small churches and NGOs
- What you see in your bank statement

**Accrual basis accounting** — record when the transaction is *earned or incurred*, even if cash hasn't moved yet.
- More accurate picture of financial position
- Required for larger organizations and grant reporting
- Example: a bill arrives in December, paid in January — accrual records it in December

> Most small and medium mission organizations start on cash basis. Clariva is designed around real cash transactions — every inflow and outflow is recorded when the money moves — making it naturally suited to cash basis accounting, with the structure to grow into accrual reporting.

---

## Balances

A **balance** is the net total of an account at a given point in time.

- A bank account with ₦200,000 received and ₦150,000 paid out has a **balance of ₦50,000**
- A salary expense account with three months of payments recorded has a **balance of total salaries paid**

Your organization's **total cash position** is the sum of all bank account balances. This is one of the most important numbers you can know.

# Module 3: Organizing Your Finances

## Structure Is Not Bureaucracy — It Is Stewardship

One of the most common mistakes mission organizations make is recording transactions without structure. Money comes in, money goes out, and it all goes into one undifferentiated pile.

The result: you know how much you have, but you have no idea where it came from or what it was used for. You cannot answer your board's questions. You cannot produce a report by programme. You cannot know if your outreach budget is overspent.

Organizing your finances is what turns raw transactions into meaningful information.

---

## The Chart of Accounts

A **Chart of Accounts (COA)** is the master list of every account your organization uses to classify financial activity. Think of it as the filing system for your money.

Every transaction gets assigned to one (or more) accounts from this list. A well-designed chart of accounts answers the question: *"What was this money for?"*

### Standard Account Categories for Mission Organizations

**Income accounts:**
- Tithes & Offerings
- Designated Donations (by project or fund)
- Grants Received
- Fundraising Income
- Interest Income

**Expense accounts:**
- Personnel (Salaries, Wages, Benefits)
- Facilities (Rent, Utilities, Maintenance)
- Ministry & Programmes (by activity)
- Administration (Office, Communication, Software)
- Travel & Transport
- Missions & Outreach

**Asset accounts:**
- Bank Accounts (one per physical account)
- Petty Cash
- Receivables (money owed to you)
- Equipment (vehicles, technology)

**Liability accounts:**
- Accounts Payable (bills you owe)
- Loans
- Deferred Income (money received for future periods)

**Net Assets (Equity):**
- Unrestricted Net Assets (general fund balance)
- Temporarily Restricted (designated for specific purpose/period)
- Permanently Restricted (endowment or permanent funds)

---

## Categories in Practice

Not every organization needs the full structure above from day one. The principle is:

> **Your categories should reflect the questions your leadership asks.**

If your pastor asks "How much did we spend on outreach this quarter?" — you need an Outreach category.
If your board asks "How much has come in as building fund donations?" — you need a Building Fund income category.
If you need to report to a grant provider by programme area — you need programme-level expense categories.

Design your chart of accounts around the decisions that need to be made.

---

## Account Numbering (Optional but Recommended)

Many organizations number their accounts for easy reference:

| Range | Account Type |
|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities |
| 3000–3999 | Net Assets / Equity |
| 4000–4999 | Income |
| 5000–5999 | Expenses |

This numbering convention is standard across accounting systems worldwide and makes reports easier to read and compare year over year.

---

## The Consistency Principle

Once you set up your chart of accounts, **use it consistently**.

One of the most important accounting principles is **consistency** — applying the same methods and classifications from period to period. This allows you to:
- Compare this month to last month
- Compare this year to last year
- Spot trends over time
- Produce reports your board can trust

Changing categories mid-year without documentation breaks comparability and undermines the integrity of your records.

# Module 4: Recording Every Transaction

## The Complete Record

An accounting record is only as good as its completeness. One unrecorded transaction can throw off your bank reconciliation. One missing receipt can undermine an entire audit. One undocumented payment can look like misappropriation — even when it wasn't.

The standard in accounting is clear:

> **Every financial transaction must be recorded. Every record must be supported by a source document.**

This module covers how to do that well.

---

## Source Documents

A **source document** is the original evidence that a transaction took place. Before any entry is made in your books, there should be a corresponding source document.

| Transaction Type | Source Document |
|---|---|
| Donation received | Giving slip, bank deposit slip, transfer confirmation |
| Bill paid | Invoice, receipt, supplier statement |
| Salary paid | Payroll record, signed payslip |
| Petty cash spent | Petty cash voucher, receipt |
| Bank transfer | Bank statement, transfer confirmation |
| Grant received | Grant agreement, payment advice |

The accounting principle of **verifiability** requires that any transaction can be traced back to its source document. If it cannot, it should not be in your books.

---

## The Journal Entry

In traditional bookkeeping, every transaction is first recorded in a **journal** — a chronological log of all financial activity. Each journal entry includes:
- The date
- The accounts affected (debit and credit)
- The amounts
- A brief description
- A reference to the source document

**Example — Offering received:**

| Date | Account | Debit | Credit | Description |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2026-06-15 | Bank — Main Account | ₦180,000 | | Sunday offering deposit |
| 2026-06-15 | Tithes & Offerings Income | | ₦180,000 | Sunday offering deposit |

Both sides balance. The record is complete.

In modern systems like Clariva, you do not write journal entries manually — the system creates them as you record transactions. But understanding the underlying structure helps you know what the system is doing.

---

## Bank Reconciliation

**Bank reconciliation** is one of the most important controls in any financial system. It is the process of comparing your internal records to your bank statement to ensure they match.

### Why Reconcile?

- Catches recording errors (wrong amounts, missed entries)
- Detects unauthorised transactions or fraud
- Confirms outstanding cheques have cleared
- Verifies deposits were received as expected

### The Reconciliation Process

1. Obtain your bank statement for the period
2. Compare each entry on the statement to your internal records
3. Identify and explain any differences:
   - **Outstanding deposits** — recorded in your books, not yet on the statement
   - **Outstanding payments** — paid out, not yet cleared the bank
   - **Bank charges** — on the statement, not yet in your books
   - **Errors** — in either your records or (rarely) the bank's
4. After accounting for all differences, your adjusted book balance should equal your adjusted bank balance

The **materiality principle** applies here: even small discrepancies should be investigated. A ₦500 difference today can signal a ₦50,000 problem tomorrow.

---

## Receipts and Evidence

Every outflow from your organization should be supported by a receipt or equivalent evidence. This is not just good practice — in many jurisdictions, it is a legal requirement for registered organizations.

A proper receipt should show:
- Date of transaction
- Vendor or payee name
- Description of what was purchased
- Amount paid
- Payment method

Keeping receipts in a disorganized pile is not enough. They must be filed in a way that allows any transaction to be retrieved and verified within minutes.

---

## The Import Pipeline

For organizations that use bank accounts (which should be all of them), the most efficient way to record transactions is to **import directly from bank statements** rather than entering each transaction manually.

This approach:
- Eliminates transcription errors
- Ensures completeness (nothing is skipped)
- Produces an automatic first draft of your records
- Creates a direct link between your books and your bank

The import process typically works like this:
1. Download your bank statement (usually as CSV or Excel)
2. Import into your financial system
3. Review and categorize each transaction
4. Attach source documents (receipts, invoices) where required
5. Reconcile — confirm the imported total matches your statement balance

# Module 5: Designated Funds and Donor Trust

## The Most Sacred Obligation in Nonprofit Finance

When a donor gives to a specific cause — a building project, a mission trip, a scholarship fund, a children's programme — they are not making a general contribution. They are placing a specific trust in you.

> **Designated giving must be used for its stated purpose. This is not just an ethical standard — it is a legal obligation in most jurisdictions.**

Failing to honour donor designations is one of the most serious failures a mission organization can commit. It damages trust, exposes leadership to legal liability, and can permanently destroy the organization's reputation.

Fund accounting — the practice of tracking designated money separately — is the accounting discipline built to prevent this.

---

## Fund Accounting

**Fund accounting** is the system used by nonprofits, churches, and government entities to account for money that is restricted to a specific purpose.

Unlike commercial accounting, where the primary goal is measuring profit, fund accounting's primary goal is demonstrating that money was used as intended.

### Types of Funds

**Unrestricted funds** — money the organization can use for any purpose consistent with its mission.
- General operating income (undesignated tithes, offerings)
- Fundraising proceeds without specific restrictions

**Temporarily restricted funds** — money that must be used for a specific purpose or within a specific time period.
- Building fund donations
- Mission trip contributions
- Project-specific grants
- Annual campaign gifts

**Permanently restricted funds** — money that must be held indefinitely; only the income it generates may be spent.
- Endowments
- Permanent memorials

The standard for fund accounting is set by bodies like the **International Financial Reporting Standards (IFRS) for SMEs**, and for US organizations, the **Financial Accounting Standards Board (FASB) ASC 958** for nonprofits. The principle is universal: track restricted money separately.

---

## Practical Fund Tracking

In practice, fund accounting means:

1. **Tag each inflow** with its fund designation at the point of receipt
2. **Track the balance** of each fund separately — not just the total bank balance
3. **Record each outflow** against the fund it was drawn from
4. **Report on each fund** — showing beginning balance, inflows, outflows, and ending balance
5. **Never use restricted funds for unrestricted purposes** without donor consent and proper documentation

### Example: Building Fund

| Date | Description | In | Out | Balance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Jan 1 | Opening balance | | | ₦0 |
| Jan 15 | Member donation — Building | ₦500,000 | | ₦500,000 |
| Feb 3 | Member donation — Building | ₦200,000 | | ₦700,000 |
| Mar 10 | Architect fees — Building phase 1 | | ₦250,000 | ₦450,000 |
| Mar 31 | Closing balance | | | **₦450,000** |

The organization may have ₦2,000,000 total in the bank — but only ₦450,000 of that is available for building purposes. The rest belongs to other funds.

---

## Budget Allocations

A **budget** is a financial plan — a commitment made in advance about how money will be received and spent over a given period.

### Why Budgets Matter

Budgets serve multiple functions:
- **Planning** — forces leadership to think ahead about income and expenses
- **Control** — provides a target against which actual spending is measured
- **Accountability** — makes overspending visible and subject to review
- **Communication** — tells the congregation or board what the organization intends to do

### Budget vs. Actual Reporting

One of the most powerful reports in nonprofit finance is the **Budget vs. Actual** report. For each category, it shows:
- What was budgeted
- What actually happened
- The variance (difference)
- The variance as a percentage

This report answers the question: *"Are we on track?"*

| Category | Budget | Actual | Variance |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tithes & Offerings | ₦1,200,000 | ₦1,050,000 | −₦150,000 (−12.5%) |
| Staff Salaries | ₦600,000 | ₦600,000 | ₦0 |
| Building Maintenance | ₦100,000 | ₦143,000 | −₦43,000 (−43%) |
| Outreach Ministry | ₦150,000 | ₦88,000 | +₦62,000 (+41%) |

Variances are not automatically bad — but they require explanation and leadership attention.

---

## Fund Transfers

Sometimes money needs to move between funds or between bank accounts. A **fund transfer** is not an income or expense — it is a movement of existing assets.

**Example:** Moving ₦200,000 from the main account to the building savings account.
- The organization's total assets do not change
- But the location and designation of the money has changed

Transfers must be recorded explicitly — not buried as unexplained debits and credits in your records.

# Module 6: Reading Your Numbers

## Financial Reports Are Not Just for Accountants

Many church and ministry leaders treat financial reports as something to hand off — to the treasurer, the accountant, the finance committee. This is a mistake.

Financial reports are the story of your organization told in numbers. A leader who cannot read that story is flying partially blind — making decisions about staff, programmes, and future plans without the full picture.

This module gives you the literacy to read your numbers confidently.

---

## The Three Core Financial Statements

Every organization — commercial or nonprofit — produces three core financial statements. Understanding these is the foundation of financial literacy.

---

### 1. The Income and Expenditure Statement
*(Also called: Income Statement, Statement of Activities, Profit & Loss)*

This statement answers: **"What came in and what went out over a period of time?"**

It covers a specific period — a month, a quarter, a year. It shows:
- Total income by source
- Total expenses by category
- The **surplus** (income exceeded expenses) or **deficit** (expenses exceeded income)

**Example — January 2026:**

| | Amount |
|---|---|
| **INCOME** | |
| Tithes & Offerings | ₦1,050,000 |
| Building Fund Donations | ₦200,000 |
| Grant Received | ₦500,000 |
| **Total Income** | **₦1,750,000** |
| | |
| **EXPENSES** | |
| Staff Salaries | ₦600,000 |
| Building Maintenance | ₦143,000 |
| Outreach Ministry | ₦88,000 |
| Administration | ₦45,000 |
| **Total Expenses** | **₦876,000** |
| | |
| **Net Surplus** | **₦874,000** |

**What to look for:**
- Is income growing or declining over time?
- Which expense categories are increasing?
- Is the organization consistently running surpluses or deficits?
- Are designated income sources being matched by designated expenditures?

---

### 2. The Balance Sheet (Statement of Financial Position)

This statement answers: **"What does the organization own and owe at a specific point in time?"**

Unlike the income statement (which covers a period), the balance sheet is a snapshot — a photograph of financial position on a specific date.

**Example — as at 31 January 2026:**

| ASSETS | |
|---|---|
| Bank — Main Account | ₦1,200,000 |
| Bank — Building Fund Account | ₦450,000 |
| Petty Cash | ₦25,000 |
| **Total Assets** | **₦1,675,000** |

| LIABILITIES | |
|---|---|
| Supplier Invoice Unpaid | ₦80,000 |
| **Total Liabilities** | **₦80,000** |

| NET ASSETS | |
|---|---|
| Unrestricted Net Assets | ₦1,145,000 |
| Restricted — Building Fund | ₦450,000 |
| **Total Net Assets** | **₦1,595,000** |

| **Total Liabilities + Net Assets** | **₦1,675,000** |

The accounting equation holds: Assets (₦1,675,000) = Liabilities (₦80,000) + Net Assets (₦1,595,000).

**What to look for:**
- Is the organization's net asset position improving over time?
- Are liabilities manageable relative to assets?
- Is cash sufficient to cover near-term obligations?
- Are restricted funds properly separated from unrestricted?

---

### 3. The Cash Flow Statement

This statement answers: **"Where did our cash come from and where did it go?"**

Even a profitable organization can run out of cash if income arrives later than expenses are due. The cash flow statement tracks the actual movement of money — not just when transactions are earned or incurred.

For most small mission organizations operating on cash basis, the income statement closely mirrors cash flow. As organizations grow and take on more complexity (grants, deferred income, loans), cash flow becomes an increasingly critical tool.

---

## Budget vs. Actual Report

As covered in Module 5, the Budget vs. Actual report is one of the most practical reports for organizational decision-making. It answers: **"Are we on track with our financial plan?"**

This report should be reviewed monthly by leadership and quarterly by the board.

---

## Key Financial Indicators

Beyond the formal statements, watch these indicators regularly:

**Cash runway** — how many months of operating expenses can you cover with current cash?
- Formula: Total Cash ÷ Monthly Operating Expenses
- Target: minimum 3 months for healthy organizations

**Income concentration** — what percentage of income comes from one source?
- High concentration (>50% from one donor or grant) = high risk

**Expense ratio by category** — what percentage of total expenses goes to personnel, programmes, administration?
- Mission-driven organizations should keep administration costs proportionate to programme spend

**Year-over-year growth** — is income growing at pace with expenses?

---

## The Principle of Disclosure

Good financial reporting is not just about producing numbers — it is about providing enough information for readers to understand the organization's financial reality.

The **disclosure principle** in accounting requires that any information that could affect a reader's interpretation of the financial statements should be disclosed. For mission organizations, this includes:
- Significant restrictions on funds
- Contingent liabilities (potential future obligations)
- Unusual or non-recurring transactions
- Related-party transactions (payments to leadership or their family members)

Transparency in reporting is not a vulnerability — it is the foundation of donor trust.

# Module 7: Your Financial Team

## Accountability Is Not Distrust — It Is Wisdom

One of the most common mistakes in mission organization finance is concentrating all financial responsibility in one person. This happens for understandable reasons: the treasurer is trustworthy, they have done it for years, everyone knows them.

But good financial governance is not about distrust. It is about protecting everyone — the organization, the donors, the leadership, and the person handling the money.

When one person controls everything, everyone is vulnerable.

---

## The Principle of Segregation of Duties

**Segregation of duties** is one of the foundational internal controls in accounting. The principle is simple:

> **No single person should have complete control over any financial transaction from beginning to end.**

At minimum, separate these functions:

| Function | What it involves | Should NOT be same person as |
|---|---|---|
| **Authorization** | Approving payments or commitments | Recording, Custody |
| **Recording** | Entering transactions into the books | Authorization, Custody |
| **Custody** | Physical control of cash or assets | Recording, Authorization |
| **Reconciliation** | Checking records against bank statements | Recording |

**Practical example for a small church:**
- The treasurer records transactions and reconciles the bank
- The pastor or a board member authorizes significant payments
- The offering is counted by two or more people together
- The board reviews and approves financial reports monthly

Even with a small team, these separations dramatically reduce both the risk of fraud and the risk of undetected errors.

---

## Roles in a Financial Team

### Common Financial Roles

**Treasurer / Finance Manager**
- Maintains day-to-day records
- Prepares financial reports
- Manages bank accounts and payments
- Performs bank reconciliation

**Finance Committee / Board**
- Reviews and approves budgets
- Approves significant expenditures
- Reviews financial reports
- Provides oversight accountability

**Authorizing Officers**
- Sign off on payments above agreed thresholds
- Approve exceptions and unusual transactions

**Auditor (Internal or External)**
- Reviews records for accuracy and compliance
- Independent from day-to-day financial operations
- Provides assurance to the board and donors

---

## Access Control

Not everyone in an organization needs access to all financial information. **Access control** — determining who can see and do what — is both a security measure and a governance tool.

In a well-structured system:
- **Administrators** can configure the system and manage users
- **Finance staff** can record and edit transactions
- **Viewers** (board members, senior leadership) can see reports but not change records
- **External auditors** may have read-only access for a specified period

Limiting access is not about withholding information from legitimate stakeholders — it is about ensuring that every action in the system is traceable to the right person.

---

## The Audit Trail

An **audit trail** is a chronological record of every action taken in a financial system — who did what, to which record, and when.

A complete audit trail shows:
- Who created each transaction
- Who modified it (and what the old values were)
- Who deleted it (if deletions are permitted)
- When each action occurred

Audit trails serve multiple purposes:
1. **Fraud detection** — unusual patterns become visible
2. **Error correction** — you can trace back to find where something went wrong
3. **Accountability** — every person's actions are attributable to them
4. **Compliance** — demonstrates due diligence to auditors and regulators

The **materiality principle** applies to audit trails too: even small changes should be logged. A ₦500 change to a record might be innocent — or it might be the beginning of a larger pattern.

## Financial Governance Policies

Every organization should have written policies governing financial behaviour. These do not need to be complex, but they should cover:

**Spending authorization levels**
- Who can approve payments under ₦50,000? Under ₦500,000? Over ₦1,000,000?
- Does anything require dual authorization?

**Petty cash management**
- What is the float limit?
- What receipts are required?
- How often is it reconciled?

**Bank account signatories**
- Who can sign cheques or authorize transfers?
- Is dual signature required above certain amounts?

**Expense reimbursement**
- What receipts must be submitted?
- Who approves reimbursements for leadership?

**Asset disposal**
- Who authorizes the sale or disposal of organizational assets?

These policies, combined with proper accounting records, are what allow an organization to say — honestly and demonstrably — that its money is being handled with integrity.
