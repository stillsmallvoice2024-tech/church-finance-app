# Module 3: Organizing Your Finances

## Structure Is Not Bureaucracy — It Is Stewardship

One of the most common mistakes mission organizations make is recording transactions without structure. Money comes in, money goes out, and it all goes into one undifferentiated pile.

The result: you know how much you have, but you have no idea where it came from or what it was used for. You cannot answer your board's questions. You cannot produce a report by programme. You cannot know if your outreach budget is overspent.

Organizing your finances is what turns raw transactions into meaningful information.

---

## The Chart of Accounts

A **Chart of Accounts (COA)** is the master list of every account your organization uses to classify financial activity. Think of it as the filing system for your money.

Every transaction gets assigned to one (or more) accounts from this list. A well-designed chart of accounts answers the question: *"What was this money for?"*

### Standard Account Categories for Mission Organizations

**Income accounts:**
- Tithes & Offerings
- Designated Donations (by project or fund)
- Grants Received
- Fundraising Income
- Interest Income

**Expense accounts:**
- Personnel (Salaries, Wages, Benefits)
- Facilities (Rent, Utilities, Maintenance)
- Ministry & Programmes (by activity)
- Administration (Office, Communication, Software)
- Travel & Transport
- Missions & Outreach

**Asset accounts:**
- Bank Accounts (one per physical account)
- Petty Cash
- Receivables (money owed to you)
- Equipment (vehicles, technology)

**Liability accounts:**
- Accounts Payable (bills you owe)
- Loans
- Deferred Income (money received for future periods)

**Net Assets (Equity):**
- Unrestricted Net Assets (general fund balance)
- Temporarily Restricted (designated for specific purpose/period)
- Permanently Restricted (endowment or permanent funds)

---

## Categories in Practice

Not every organization needs the full structure above from day one. The principle is:

> **Your categories should reflect the questions your leadership asks.**

If your pastor asks "How much did we spend on outreach this quarter?" — you need an Outreach category.
If your board asks "How much has come in as building fund donations?" — you need a Building Fund income category.
If you need to report to a grant provider by programme area — you need programme-level expense categories.

Design your chart of accounts around the decisions that need to be made.

---

## Account Numbering (Optional but Recommended)

Many organizations number their accounts for easy reference:

| Range | Account Type |
|---|---|
| 1000–1999 | Assets |
| 2000–2999 | Liabilities |
| 3000–3999 | Net Assets / Equity |
| 4000–4999 | Income |
| 5000–5999 | Expenses |

This numbering convention is standard across accounting systems worldwide and makes reports easier to read and compare year over year.

---

## The Consistency Principle

Once you set up your chart of accounts, **use it consistently**.

One of the most important accounting principles is **consistency** — applying the same methods and classifications from period to period. This allows you to:
- Compare this month to last month
- Compare this year to last year
- Spot trends over time
- Produce reports your board can trust

Changing categories mid-year without documentation breaks comparability and undermines the integrity of your records.
